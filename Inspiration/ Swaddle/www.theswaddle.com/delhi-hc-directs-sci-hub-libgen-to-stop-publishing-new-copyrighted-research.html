Delhi HC Directs Sci‑Hub, Libgen To Stop Publishing New, Copyrighted Research

With academic journal subscriptions rates completely out of reach for most Indian students, these sites represent their only access to quality research.

written by
Devrupa Rakshit
published
Dec 24, 2020
XWhatsappLinkedIn
Image Credit: iStock

The Delhi High Court has directed Sci-Hub and Libgen to not publish any new articles that infringe upon the copyrights of three well-known global publications that had filed a petition against the websites for providing free access to their paywalled research — worrying researchers that this could spell the end of access to quality research in India.

Alleging that Sci-Hub and Libgen promote viewing and downloading “of original work for which plaintiffs have exclusive rights,” Elsevier, Wiley (and its Indian subsidiary) and the American Chemical Society, sought a ban on these websites. According to Hindustan Times, Sci-Hub is believed to have published about 84 million articles, while Libgen claims to host around 80 million pieces of research.

While Justice Rajiv Shakdher, who heard the plea by the trio of publishers, held that “no new articles will be uploaded or made available,” he also asked the petitioners’ counsel, “Where were your client [sic] for so many years? These sites are known by everyone.”

Even before the direction of the Delhi HC came in, researchers had begun voicing their concerns against the petition. “[Free access] provides budding scholars and researchers, especially from marginalized sections, access to knowledge in an academic world where the existing knowledge distribution and dissemination remains out of reach largely,” Richard Kamei, a doctoral candidate at TISS, Mumbai, told Scroll.in.

Kamei’s views have support. On social media, some tweeted that “Indian researchers have been the most unfortunate victims of paywall hegemony;” while one researcher lamented that a ban “would be damaging for a lot of Indian researchers… who often resort to Sci-Hub since [the papers aren’t] available elsewhere,” without a paywall; and yet another added that Sci-Hub and Libgen are the “only reason students from egregiously underfunded institutions in India manage to do quality research.”

Some legal experts, too, believe that a ban could be detrimental to society. Arul George Scaria, Associate Professor at National Law University, Delhi, who specializes in Intellectual Property Rights, wrote in The Wire that a ban could have serious socio-economic implications on the country — including jeopardizing public health if healthcare professionals aren’t able to access latest developments in medicine. He further adds that the paywalls prevent the advancement of science, saying: “Thanks to the manner in which publication giants have been controlling access to peer-reviewed scientific literature today, most of it languishes behind paywalls that require hundreds to lakhs of rupees to unlock.”

“The kind of intellectual monopoly that Elsevier, Wiley, and ACS seek is harmful for the public, and judges should refuse to grant an ex-parte preliminary injunction in such a case,” Pranesh Prakash, a law and policy researcher from India, who is an Affiliated Fellow at the Yale Law School’s Information Society Project, said. Commenting on the allegation that the policies of Sci-Hub and Libgen essentially amount to piracy, he tweeted: “Piracy is critical to access to knowledge, and especially so in India and other developing countries.”

However, the petitioners argued that under the guise of providing free access, the websites “freeload on the hard work, skill and labour” of the publishers and their authors — completely disregarding copyright laws, which were enacted to protect creators from exploitation of their work. And free access, the petitioners argue, strips the authors and publishers of the fruits of their labor.

The journals further claim that libraries at educational institutions should subscribe to them, enabling students to gain access to quality research without resorting to piracy. However, even academic institutions find subscription costs to these journals exorbitant. In the past, reputed institutions like Harvard have stated that the subscription costs of most paywalled journals are prohibitive and unaffordable. And earlier this year, MIT ended negotiations with Elsevier.

At present in India, an annual subscription to the online version of The Lancet, one of best-known medical journals globally, which is published by Elsevier, costs upwards of Rs. 11,000 for students, and above Rs. 15,000 for others. Elsevier’s operating profit margins (37.1% in 2019) and high revenues have already been subjected to criticism outside India for “mak[ing] profits based on the free labor of mathematicians and subscription fees from their institutions’ libraries, for a service that has become largely unnecessary.”

In India, too, scientists are beginning to comment on the unfair profit-making practices of the paywalled journals. “Research worldwide is mostly funded by taxpayer money, done by students on scholarships, supervised by professors and scientists, reviewed by the same community voluntarily for free,” Manabendra Saharia an assistant professor at the IIT-Delhi, noted, adding that researchers don’t get “a single cent for publishing or reviewing papers for any publisher regardless how much publishers make.” Blocking access to Sci-Hub and Libgen would be “a punitive measure against Indian researchers who already are suffering from various other issues,” Saharia concludes.

The Delhi HC has directed the defendants to file their reply to the petition by December 31, and scheduled the next hearing for January 6.
